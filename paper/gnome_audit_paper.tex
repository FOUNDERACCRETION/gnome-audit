\begin{abstract}
Machine-learning crystal discovery now reports hundreds of thousands of thermodynamically
stable materials, but these labels rest on generalized-gradient-approximation density
functional theory. GNoME reports that 84\% of its PBE-stable predictions retain negative
phase-separation energies under the r$^2$SCAN meta-GGA, a single aggregate figure. We
resolve that figure by chemistry using the 139{,}541 GNoME materials released with
r$^2$SCAN recalculations. Because the dataset is substitution-generated, these materials
are not independent observations: they fall into 8{,}429 structural-prototype clusters with
an intra-cluster correlation of 0.14, a design effect of 3.1, and an effective sample size
near 44{,}000. Flip-rate intervals are cluster bootstrap intervals and regression intervals
use a cluster-robust sandwich estimator, which inflates standard errors by 2.8 times. Holding element count fixed,
the verdict-flip rate ranges from 4.0\% [3.2, 4.8] to 30.7\% [27.6, 33.8] across disjoint
composition cells, and per element from 1.4\% (Ac) to 42.8\% (Fe), with all 83 element-level
contrasts surviving Benjamini--Hochberg correction. The gradient is not an artefact of the
MP2020 Hubbard-$U$ corrections applied to PBE but not to r$^2$SCAN: restricted to
chemistries where no $U$ is applied, materials containing Co, Cr, Fe, Mn, Mo, Ni, V or W
flip at 21.9\%, against 7.2\% for materials whose only 3$d$ elements are Sc, Ti, Cu or Zn,
indistinguishable from materials with no 3$d$ element at all (7.4\%). The iron excess over
baseline is smallest in oxides (+5 points), the one class where PBE receives a $U$
correction, and largest in borides, pnictides and intermetallics (+34 to +41 points), where
it does not: the correction scheme does not generate the disagreement but partially
suppresses it where applied. The underlying quantity is a directional shift, not noise:
r$^2$SCAN moves 3$d$ oxides and 3$d$ pnictides toward instability by a mean of +11 and
+10 meV/atom and moves plain intermetallics toward stability by $-12$ meV/atom, with
comparable scatter in every cell. Open-shell $d$ content is the strongest observed
predictor of disagreement, with a cluster-robust odds ratio of 3.45 [3.13, 3.80]; we do not
claim an identified mechanism. The gradient survives within every PBE hull-distance bin, is
not a band-gap effect, and is not an artefact of which materials were selected for
recomputation; on the Materials Project side, where r$^2$SCAN hull completeness can be
measured, the open-shell $d$ excess is unchanged across completeness terciles. A matched comparison against Materials Project
entries computed at both levels reverses the aggregate comparison: GNoME flips less often
overall (13.0\% against 14.9\%) but more often within oxide and 3$d$-containing cells.
Allocating a fixed r$^2$SCAN recomputation budget by chemistry recovers 37.1\%
$\pm$ 0.9\% of disagreements at a 20\% budget against 20.1\% for uniform sampling, over 20
cluster-wise splits. All results apply to materials with at most four elements: quinary
compositions, which GNoME highlights as a principal novelty, are essentially unvalidated at
the meta-GGA level (229 of 177{,}392 recomputed).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Computational materials discovery has shifted from enumerating candidates to generating
them. GNoME reported 381{,}000 new entries on the convex hull for a total of 421{,}000
stable crystals \citep{merchant2023}; subsequent releases brought the public dataset to
554{,}054 entries. Generative models such as MatterGen \citep{zeni2025} and expanded
databases such as Alexandria \citep{schmidt2022} have followed. Every one of those
stability verdicts is a density functional theory (DFT) calculation at the
Perdew--Burke--Ernzerhof (PBE) level \citep{pbe1996}, executed in VASP \citep{vasp1996}
with Materials Project-compatible settings \citep{jain2013} including the MP2020 empirical
anion and Hubbard-$U$ corrections \citep{wang2021mp2020}.

That GGA formation energies carry errors of order 0.1 eV/atom is well established. Kirklin
et al.\ report a mean absolute error of 0.136 eV/atom against 1{,}670 experimental
formation energies, falling to 0.081 eV/atom with elemental fitting, against an
experiment-versus-experiment MAE of 0.082 eV/atom \citep{kirklin2015}. What has not been
measured is how those errors distribute across the chemical space that generative discovery
actually occupies.

The distinction is practical. A laboratory choosing which predicted compound to attempt
cannot act on an aggregate error rate. A database maintainer deciding where to spend a
finite higher-fidelity recomputation budget faces the same problem at scale. And a
benchmark that treats a PBE hull as ground truth \citep{riebesell2025} implicitly assumes
the label noise is uniform across chemistry.

GNoME's authors anticipated the question. They recomputed a subset with the r$^2$SCAN
meta-GGA \citep{furness2020} and reported that 84\% of discovered binaries and ternaries
retain negative phase-separation energies, with 86.8\% of tested quaternaries remaining
stable on the r$^2$SCAN hull \citep{merchant2023}. Supplementary Note 2 observes that the
discrepancy concentrates near the hull and that the same effect appears in the Materials
Project and OQMD, but gives no breakdown by chemistry. The authors released the underlying
r$^2$SCAN data with the statement that it is provided ``expectantly serving as a foundation
for analysing discrepancies between functional choices.''

\subsection{What this paper measures, and what it does not}\label{sec:scope}

We measure \emph{disagreement between two computational methods}. Three concepts must stay
separate:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Functional disagreement.} PBE and r$^2$SCAN assign different stability
classifications. This is what we observe.
\item \textbf{Computational accuracy.} One functional is closer to the truth for a given
material. We do not establish this.
\item \textbf{Experimental stability.} The material is thermodynamically stable in reality.
We say nothing about this.
\end{enumerate}

We therefore use the term \emph{flip} throughout, and avoid describing flipped labels as
``incorrect''. Where we discuss practical consequences, the operative quantity is the share
of labels that a higher-fidelity recomputation would revise --- which is a statement about
what the expensive calculation would say, not about physical reality. This distinction
constrains every claim that follows, including the budget model in
Section~\ref{sec:budget}, whose payoff is measured in disagreements surfaced rather than
errors corrected.

\subsection{Questions and contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Q1.} Does the flip rate vary systematically with chemistry, controlling for
element count, and what drives the variation?
\item \textbf{Q2.} Do AI-discovered materials flip at a different rate than previously
catalogued materials in matched chemistry?
\item \textbf{Q3.} Given a fixed recomputation budget, how much more disagreement is
surfaced by allocating by chemistry rather than uniformly?
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions: (i) a chemistry- and element-resolved map of functional-level
stability-verdict disagreement on an AI-generated materials database; (ii) controlled tests
showing the gradient survives stratification by hull distance and is not produced by band
gap, structure, cell size or recomputation selection, and that the empirical correction
scheme partially suppresses rather than creates it; (ii$'$) the observation that the
gradient arises from a chemistry-dependent sign in the PBE-to-r$^2$SCAN energy shift; (iii) a matched comparison against previously catalogued materials
showing that the published aggregate comparison conceals a sign reversal; (iv) a
verification-budget model evaluated over repeated cluster-wise splits; and (v) the
observation that the compositional regime GNoME advertises most prominently is almost
entirely unvalidated at the meta-GGA level.

The analysis was pre-registered: decision rules, including the threshold that would have
caused us to abandon the chemistry-resolved framing, were committed to version control
before any stratified result was computed.

\section{Related work}

\subsection{Functional comparisons on known materials}

Kingsbury et al.\ benchmarked r$^2$SCAN against SCAN and PBEsol across roughly 6{,}000
solids and found r$^2$SCAN more accurate than either for formation energies
\citep{kingsbury2022prm}, and developed the mixing scheme the Materials Project uses to
place PBE(+$U$) and r$^2$SCAN energies on a single phase diagram \citep{kingsbury2022npj}.

Two results bear directly on what follows. Kothakonda et al.\ tested r$^2$SCAN
thermochemistry on more than 1{,}000 solids and concluded that for transition metals,
intermetallics and weakly bound solids, GGAs are comparable to meta-GGAs, and that for
Type 2 decomposition enthalpies PBE is slightly more accurate \citep{kothakonda2023}.
Bartel et al.\ reached a compatible verdict from the reaction side: across 646
decomposition reactions that are not simply formation reactions, PBE (70 meV/atom) and SCAN
(59 meV/atom) perform similarly \citep{bartel2019}. Both predict, on physical grounds, that
intermetallics should be the chemistry in which the two functionals most nearly
\emph{agree}. Section~\ref{sec:cells} confirms this on an AI-generated set, which serves as
a consistency check on our pipeline as well as a result.

\subsection{Correction schemes and their pathologies}

The MP2020 scheme quantifies uncertainty in DFT energy corrections and shows that many
polymorphs classified as unstable may be stable once that uncertainty is propagated
\citep{wang2021mp2020}. Warford, Thiemann and Cs\'anyi argue that applying $U$ selectively
--- only when oxygen or fluorine is present --- creates two mutually incompatible
potential-energy surfaces within one dataset \citep{warford2026}. Section~\ref{sec:u}
exploits that boundary as a natural experiment.

\subsection{Chemistry-resolved error analysis}

Sun et al.\ data-mined 29{,}902 observed phases to produce a chemistry-resolved scale of
metastability \citep{sun2016}. Yu et al.\ map universal MLIP formation-energy error per
element across 19{,}998 unary and binary phases \citep{yu2024}. Both stratify a
computed-energy error by chemistry; neither addresses functional-level verdict flips, and
neither concerns a generative dataset.

\subsection{Critiques of AI-generated catalogues}

Cheetham and Seshadri questioned how many GNoME entries are simultaneously novel, credible
and useful \citep{cheetham2024}. Liu and Meng report that metals constitute over 60\% of
GNoME and that Ho, Tb, Rh and Er are over-represented relative to the Materials Project
\citep{liu2024}; that compositional skew matters for interpreting aggregate comparisons
(Section~\ref{sec:known}). Leeman et al.\ examined the companion autonomous-synthesis work
and concluded that no new materials were demonstrated in it \citep{leeman2024}; the
underlying paper has since been corrected \citep{szymanski2023,szymanski2026}. None of
these addresses label reliability.

\subsection{Flip-rate framing}

The closest prior framing is Reents, Bercx and Pizzi, whose supplementary material plots a
PBEsol-versus-SCAN stability flip rate against threshold for Alexandria
\citep{reents2026}. That analysis is aggregate, concerns a different database and
functional pair, serves to validate a machine-learned correction rather than to
characterise a generative dataset, reports no chemistry resolution, and gives its numerical
values only in a figure. An et al.\ re-screened an ML-generated set, finding 298 of 603
curated structures remained PBE-stable on complete hulls and 109 of 166 dynamically stable
candidates survived a SCAN refinement \citep{an2026}; they excluded 142 rare-earth
compounds outright because of $f$-electron and pseudopotential uncertainty. Huang et al.\
map PBE formation energies onto the r$^2$SCAN scale across 380{,}190 paired MatPES entries
\citep{huang2026,kaplan2025} but verify stability verdicts for only six binary systems.

We are not aware of a prior chemistry- or element-resolved verdict-flip analysis on GNoME
or any other AI-generated database, but we state this as the result of our own literature
search rather than as an established priority claim, and the contribution does not depend
on it. Stated without reference to priority, what this paper adds is a chemistry- and
element-resolved analysis of functional-level stability-verdict disagreement in the
recomputed GNoME subset, with explicit treatment of correlated substitution-generated
structures and of recomputation selection, a matched comparison against previously
catalogued materials, and an evaluation of chemistry-aware budget allocation. Each
component has relatives in the literature cited above; the combination, and its application
to a generative dataset, is what is new here.

\section{Data and methods}

\subsection{Datasets}

\paragraph{GNoME.} Two files from the public release \citep{merchant2023}:
\texttt{stable\_materials\_summary.csv} (554{,}054 unique material IDs with PBE-level
decomposition energies, band gaps, space groups and cell information) and
\texttt{stable\_materials\_r2scan.csv} (139{,}541 unique IDs with r$^2$SCAN
recalculations). The release notes state that not all materials remain stable under the
r$^2$SCAN metric, and we confirm this: positive decomposition energies are present
throughout, so the file is not censored to survivors. This matters because the
corresponding Materials Project release of GNoME entries appears to have been selected on
r$^2$SCAN stability and cannot serve the same purpose.

Each file reports decomposition energy against several reference sets: against the
Materials Project hull, against MP and OQMD jointly, and against a set the release labels
``All'' and does not further define. We take the MP-referenced column as primary because it
reproduces the published aggregate, report the MP+OQMD variant as a sensitivity check, and
examine the ``All'' column separately in Section~\ref{sec:allref} because it gives a
materially different result.

Of the 117{,}043 recomputed materials that can be matched to a PBE decomposition energy in
the summary file, 25 have a positive PBE value and were therefore not stable to begin with.
They cannot flip and are excluded from every analysis that uses PBE energies. They are
0.02\% of the set and no reported number changes.

\paragraph{Known materials.} We query the Materials Project thermo endpoint for entries
carrying both \texttt{GGA\_GGA+U} and \texttt{r2SCAN} thermodynamic data. This enumeration
value is case-sensitive: the string \texttt{R2SCAN} returns zero documents without raising
an error, a silent failure mode that would make a replication attempt look like a
refutation. We retrieved 37{,}123 r$^2$SCAN and 152{,}426 GGA+$U$ documents; 35{,}176
material IDs carry both, of which 22{,}756 are stable at the GGA+$U$ level and therefore
capable of flipping. The \texttt{r2SCAN} thermo type is distinct from the Materials
Project's mixed GGA/GGA+$U$/r$^2$SCAN scheme \citep{mpmixingdocs}: its hull is built from
whichever r$^2$SCAN entries exist in a chemical system, with no requirement that every
competing phase has been recomputed. We measure how complete those hulls are in
Section~\ref{sec:allref}.

\subsection{Definitions}

For GNoME a material is \emph{flipped} when its r$^2$SCAN decomposition energy exceeds
zero. For Materials Project entries, where \texttt{energy\_above\_hull} is non-negative by
construction, we define stability as $E_{\text{hull}} \le 10^{-6}$ eV/atom and flipping as
GGA+$U$-stable with $E_{\text{hull}}^{\text{r}^2\text{SCAN}} > 10^{-6}$.

We classify each composition into one of seven mutually exclusive anion classes by
priority: oxide (contains O); halide (F, Cl, Br or I, no O); chalcogenide (S, Se or Te,
none of the above); pnictide (N, P, As, Sb or Bi, none of the above); hydride (H, none of
the above); boride/carbide/silicide (B, C or Si, none of the above); intermetallic (none of
the above). We flag the presence of a lanthanide (La--Lu) and of a 3$d$ transition metal
(Sc--Zn). Cells are the Cartesian product of element count, anion class, lanthanide flag
and 3$d$ flag, and are disjoint by construction. Only cells with $n \ge 500$ enter the
primary analysis; per-element analyses use $n \ge 300$.

\subsection{Materials are not independent observations}\label{sec:cluster}

GNoME is substitution-generated: the authors attribute 232{,}477 of 381{,}000 stable
structures to symmetry-aware partial substitution \citep{merchant2023}. Materials sharing a
parent structure and differing by one or two ionic substitutions are not independent
samples of chemical space, and may share relaxation behaviour, competing phases and
systematic error.

We quantify this using a structural-prototype proxy: the pair (space group number,
anonymised stoichiometry), where anonymised stoichiometry replaces element identities with
sorted site counts, so that Nd$_7$Os$_1$Pr$_3$Si$_5$ becomes A1B3C5D7. The 139{,}541
recomputed materials fall into \textbf{8{,}429 such clusters}, mean size 16.6. The five
largest hold 2{,}953, 2{,}711, 2{,}374, 1{,}570 and 1{,}122 materials. Only 29.6\% of
clusters are singletons, and 87.5\% of all materials sit in clusters of ten or more.

The intra-cluster correlation of the flip outcome is $\rho = 0.138$ (ANOVA estimator).
With mean cluster size $n_0 = 16.5$ the design effect is
$1 + (n_0-1)\rho = 3.14$, implying an effective sample size near 44{,}000 rather than
139{,}541, and confidence intervals wider by a factor of about $\sqrt{3.14} = 1.77$ than
naive binomial intervals.

\textbf{All intervals in this paper are therefore cluster bootstrap intervals}: we resample
prototype clusters with replacement, not individual materials, 600 replicates for cells and
300 for elements. Across the 40 primary cells the observed widening relative to naive
intervals has median 1.82 and ranges from 1.14 to 4.65. An earlier version of this analysis
used naive intervals and overstated precision accordingly; the point estimates are
unchanged.

The prototype proxy is a choice, so we test an alternative. Clustering on anonymised
stoichiometry alone, ignoring space group, is far coarser: 2{,}186 clusters, mean size 63.8,
largest 7{,}007. The headline quaternary contrast is essentially unaffected: 30.7\%
[27.3, 34.2] against 4.0\% [3.2, 4.7] under the coarse definition, compared with
30.7\% [27.7, 33.6] against 4.0\% [3.3, 4.8] under the primary one (both from a
500-replicate re-draw; the 600-replicate primary intervals in Appendix~\ref{app:cells}
differ in the last digit). A third definition
adding the element set is too fine to be informative (mean cluster size 1.03) and we do not
use it. We report the primary definition throughout and note that results are not sensitive
to coarsening it.

\subsection{Multiple comparisons}

The per-element analysis performs 83 tests. We report Benjamini--Hochberg adjusted
$p$-values for the contrast between each element's flip rate and the global rate, computed
from the cluster bootstrap standard error. The primary cell-level comparison and the
lanthanide hypothesis were pre-registered; the per-element analysis was not, and is labelled
exploratory throughout despite surviving correction.

\subsection{Uncertainty for the regression}

Logistic regression coefficients are fitted by iteratively reweighted least squares.
Standard errors use a cluster-robust sandwich estimator,
$V = B \left(\sum_g X_g^{\top} u_g u_g^{\top} X_g\right) B$ with
$B = (X^{\top} W X)^{-1}$ and $u_g$ the score contributions of cluster $g$, clustering on
the same structural prototypes used for the bootstrap. Relative to naive analytic errors
this inflates the standard error on the open-shell $d$ coefficient by a factor of 2.77,
consistent with the design effect estimated independently in Section~\ref{sec:cluster}.

\subsection{Pre-registration}

The pre-registered primary rule was that a spread of $\ge 5$ percentage points between the
highest and lowest cell, with non-overlapping intervals at fixed element count, would
support a standalone analysis; below 3 points the result would be reported as a single
parameter elsewhere. The secondary pre-registered claim was that lanthanide-containing
materials would flip at or below the global rate. That claim was partially refuted and we
report it as such. Rules and results were committed in that order.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The aggregate figure reproduces, and conceals an order of magnitude of
variation}\label{sec:cells}

Across all 139{,}541 recomputed materials the flip rate is 13.04\% against the Materials
Project hull, 15.93\% against MP+OQMD, and 25.8\% against the most inclusive reference set.
The first reproduces the published 86.8\% quaternary survival figure to within 0.2
percentage points, validating the pipeline against the source before any new claim is made.

Resolved by chemistry at fixed element count, the picture changes entirely
(Figure~\ref{fig:chem}; full table with cluster-robust intervals in
Appendix~\ref{app:cells}). Among quaternary materials, 23 cells qualify and the flip rate
ranges from 4.0\% [3.2, 4.8] for plain intermetallics containing neither a lanthanide nor a
3$d$ metal ($n=5{,}025$) to 30.7\% [27.6, 33.8] for 3$d$-containing oxides without a
lanthanide ($n=2{,}478$). The spread of 26.7 points is more than five times the
pre-registered threshold and the intervals remain disjoint under clustering. Ternaries show
a comparable spread of 23.9 points across 17 cells.

The ordering matches what the functional-benchmarking literature predicts. Kothakonda et
al.\ find GGAs comparable to meta-GGAs precisely for transition metals and intermetallics
\citep{kothakonda2023}, and intermetallics are where we measure the lowest flip rate;
Bartel et al.\ reach a similar conclusion from decomposition reactions \citep{bartel2019}.
Our contribution is to show that this known accuracy structure propagates into a
\emph{verdict} disagreement of practical magnitude on a generative dataset, and to quantify
it cell by cell.

\subsection{Per-element rates span a factor of thirty, sevenfold among non-$f$ elements}\label{sec:element}

Computing the flip rate for every element appearing in at least 300 recomputed materials
(83 elements; Figure~\ref{fig:element}, full table in Appendix~\ref{app:element}) gives a
sharper picture. Iron-containing materials flip at 42.8\% [40.2, 46.2] ($n=5{,}762$, 1{,}218
prototype clusters). Cobalt follows at 33.2\% [30.0, 35.6], chromium at 30.7\%
[25.6, 36.8] and manganese at 25.2\% [22.8, 27.9]. At the other end, actinium-containing
materials flip at 1.4\% [1.0, 2.0], protactinium at 3.6\% [2.6, 4.8], magnesium at 5.7\%
[4.2, 7.7] and iridium at 6.2\% [5.5, 7.1].

All 83 element-level contrasts against the global rate remain significant after
Benjamini--Hochberg correction at the 0.05 level. This is unsurprising given cluster counts
in the hundreds to thousands per element, and we note it to close the multiple-comparisons
question rather than as evidence of anything in itself. We continue to treat the
per-element analysis as exploratory because it was not pre-registered.

Three cautions apply to this ranking. Per-element rates are not disjoint: an iron oxide
counts toward both Fe and O, so an element's rate is confounded with the anion classes in
which it tends to occur. Section~\ref{sec:u} addresses this by reporting the \emph{excess}
flip rate attributable to Fe, Co and Ni within each anion class, which is the quantity to
read for element-level conclusions. The bottom of the ranking is dominated by actinides
(Ac, Pa, Np), which carry the same frozen-$f$ pseudopotential caveat as lanthanides
(Section~\ref{sec:ln}), so the ``thirtyfold'' range should not be over-read; the range
among non-$f$ elements is roughly sevenfold. And the correspondence with the Materials
Project $U$ list is imperfect: nickel sits at 13.4\%, essentially the global average.
Section~\ref{sec:u} shows that nickel's excess is concentrated in anion classes where it
is more likely to carry a magnetic moment, which is consistent with magnetic open-shell
character rather than $U$-list membership being the operative variable.

\subsection{The correction scheme suppresses the disagreement where applied; it does not
create it}\label{sec:u}

PBE labels carry the MP2020 anion and Hubbard-$U$ corrections; raw r$^2$SCAN carries none.
If the gradient simply tracked where $U$ is applied, it would be an artefact of
bookkeeping. The Materials Project applies $U$ only to Co, Cr, Fe, Mn, Mo, Ni, V and W, and
only in oxides and fluorides \citep{warford2026}, which partitions the dataset into a
corrected and an uncorrected regime and lets us test the question two ways.

\paragraph{First test: restrict to the uncorrected regime.} Among compositions containing
neither oxygen nor fluorine, where no $U$ is applied under either functional, quaternary
materials containing one of the eight $U$-list elements flip at 21.9\%, those whose only
3$d$ elements are Sc, Ti, Cu or Zn at 7.2\%, and those with no 3$d$ element at 7.4\%
(Table~\ref{tab:u}). The ternary subset shows the same three-way separation. The effect is
present where no correction exists, so the correction does not generate it.

\paragraph{Second test: compare the corrected and uncorrected regimes directly.} The
sharper question is how much iron raises the flip rate \emph{within} each anion class
(Table~\ref{tab:feexcess}). The excess is smallest in oxides, where PBE+$U$ is applied, and

Cobalt shows the same pattern. Nickel, which is frequently low-spin or non-magnetic in
metallic environments, shows essentially no excess in intermetallics and a modest one in
oxides and chalcogenides.

\end{table}

\paragraph{Reading.} Together the two tests support a more specific conclusion than
``not the correction scheme.'' The correction scheme does not generate the disagreement:
it is present, and largest, where no correction is applied. But where $U$ \emph{is}
applied, in oxides, the open-shell $d$ excess shrinks from over thirty points to five. The
empirical correction is doing part of what the meta-GGA does. The largest PBE/r$^2$SCAN
disagreements in the entire dataset are open-shell 3$d$ elements in non-oxide environments,
which is precisely the regime where PBE is run with no correction and where Warford et
al.\ identify the uncorrected potential-energy surface \citep{warford2026}.

Scandium and titanium are $d^0$ in their common oxidation states and copper and zinc are
$d^{10}$; all four behave like main-group elements. Nickel's weak effect, concentrated in
anion classes where it is more likely to carry a moment, suggests the operative variable is
magnetic open-shell $d$ character rather than membership of the $U$ list as such. We state
this as the most consistent reading of the association, not as an identified mechanism.

\subsection{The gradient survives within every PBE hull-distance bin}\label{sec:hulldist}

The most obvious alternative explanation for Section~\ref{sec:cells} is proximity. Materials
close to the PBE hull need only a small energy change to cross it. If 3$d$ oxides sit
nearer the hull than intermetallics, they will flip more often for no reason connected to
the functional. Supplementary Note 2 of the source work reports that disagreement
concentrates near the hull, which makes this a live concern. Testing it was pre-registered
as robustness gate 6; we report it here.

Table~\ref{tab:hulldist} stratifies quaternary materials by PBE decomposition energy. Two
things are visible. Flip rate rises steeply toward the hull in every cell, confirming the
Note 2 observation: plain intermetallics go from 0.5\% in the $[-0.1,-0.05]$ band to 15.9\%
in $[-0.01, 0]$. And the chemistry gradient is present inside every band. In the
$[-0.05,-0.02]$ band, 3$d$ oxides flip at 23.1\% and plain intermetallics at 1.2\%; in
$[-0.01,0]$, 40.1\% against 15.9\%. The within-band spread between those two cells is
10.6, 21.8, 25.2 and 24.2 points across the four populated bands.

The cells are not differently positioned relative to the hull. Median PBE decomposition
energy is $-20.8$ meV/atom for 3$d$ oxides, $-22.4$ for plain intermetallics, $-27.3$ for
oxides without 3$d$ and $-28.1$ for 3$d$ pnictides. Hull proximity and chemistry act as
roughly independent factors, and the chemistry effect is not a proximity effect in
disguise.

This sharpens the connection to prior benchmarking. Kothakonda et al.\ and Bartel et al.\

destabilisation of 3$d$ oxides is the more accurate direction is a question about
experiment that this paper does not address.

\subsection{Nor is it an artefact of which materials were recomputed}\label{sec:ipw}

The recomputed subset is not a random sample. We model the probability of recomputation
directly, fitting a logistic model of recomputation on composition, element count, cell
size and PBE decomposition energy (linear and quadratic) across the 371{,}870
$\le$4-element materials, of which 116{,}814 (31.4\%) were recomputed.

Selection is indeed chemistry-dependent: oxides are more likely to be recomputed (odds
ratio 1.64), as are borides, carbides and silicides (1.58) and pnictides (1.43), while
lanthanide-containing materials (0.68) and larger cells (0.65 per standard deviation of
$\log$ sites) are less likely. The referee's concern is therefore well founded in its
premise.

It does not, however, change the result. Inverse-probability weighting moves the overall
$\le$4-element flip rate from 13.37\% to 12.71\%, and the spread across quaternary cells
from 22.0 to 21.9 percentage points (Table~\ref{tab:ipw}). Cell-level estimates move by at
most 2.4 points and the ordering is preserved.

\subsection{Structural and size effects are small}

By crystal system, flip rates run from 16.8\% (triclinic) to 11.1\% (hexagonal), with cubic
at 14.3\% and the rest between 12.6\% and 13.3\%. The triclinic excess is plausibly a proxy
for low-symmetry structures being harder to relax consistently, but we have not established
that. By cell size, rates rise from 11.3\% ($\le$10 sites) to 16.3\% ($>$32 sites); in the
regression below the size term is not significant once composition is controlled for.

The presence of an open-shell $d$ element multiplies the \emph{odds} of a flip by 3.45. An
odds ratio is not a risk ratio: at the reference baseline of about 6\% the corresponding
probability ratio is near 3.1, and at higher baselines it compresses further. Lanthanide
content has an odds ratio of 1.02 with an interval straddling unity. And element count

predictor of functional disagreement in this dataset, not as an identified physical
mechanism.

\subsection{The lanthanide effect reverses sign with the anion}\label{sec:ln}

Our pre-registered expectation, following the standard concern about frozen-$4f$
pseudopotentials, was that lanthanide materials would be least reliable. The concern is
well founded in the literature: An et al.\ excluded 142 rare-earth compounds on exactly
these grounds \citep{an2026}, GGA treatment of the Gd $4f^7$ shell has been argued to
render the Materials Project hull unreliable for these elements \citep{rare2026}, and
frozen-$f$ potentials are documented as appropriate only where $f$ electrons are
unimportant \citep{pseudodojo2018}.

On average the expectation fails: lanthanide materials flip at 11.8\% against a global
13.0\%. But the matched-cell comparison shows this average is misleading. Among quaternary
3$d$-containing materials, lanthanides \emph{reduce} the flip rate in halides (9.1\% against
22.4\%) and oxides (18.1\% against 30.7\%), and \emph{raise} it in pnictides (30.1\%
against 17.8\%). Among quaternaries without a 3$d$ metal, lanthanides raise the pnictide
rate from 6.0\% to 13.9\%. The sign depends on the anion.

A flip in lanthanide chemistry is not evidence that r$^2$SCAN is correct: neither
functional treats frozen-$4f$ physics properly, and the disagreement is a flag, not a
verdict.

\subsection{Matched against known materials, the aggregate comparison inverts}\label{sec:known}

This comparison carries the weakest inferential warrant in the paper, and we state its

competing phases and so understates flip rates, on either side. On the Materials Project
side we can measure completeness directly (Section~\ref{sec:allref}): the r$^2$SCAN hulls
of known oxides are the least complete of any anion class (median 37\% of competing
GGA entries recomputed) and those of intermetallics the most complete (64\%). The known
oxide flip rate is therefore the most understated, and the +17-point gap in 3$d$ oxides is
a lower bound with respect to this bias. In intermetallics the Materials Project hull is
comparatively complete while GNoME's is, by the ``All''-reference evidence, the least
complete of any class, so part of GNoME's $-7.7$-point advantage there may be
incompleteness on the GNoME side rather than agreement. Matching the two sets on distance
below the PBE hull, which Section~\ref{sec:hulldist} shows matters within GNoME, is not
possible: the Materials Project reports \texttt{energy\_above\_hull} floored at zero for
stable entries and exposes no sub-hull margin. We note only that the GNoME-side gradient
holds among ternaries within 20 meV/atom of the hull, where the known-materials cells are
concentrated: 11.6\% for plain intermetallics ($n=3{,}101$) against 38.5\% for oxides
($n=304$), 37.2\% for 3$d$ pnictides ($n=400$) and 45.9\% for 3$d$ borides, carbides and
silicides ($n=292$). The two sets are matched on composition class and element count only.
They differ in structural complexity, distance from the hull, provenance, phase-diagram
completeness and the process by which each was selected for r$^2$SCAN recomputation. The
defensible claim is therefore: \emph{within the analysed datasets, functional-level
disagreement differs between GNoME and previously catalogued materials in a
chemistry-dependent manner, and the direction of the aggregate comparison differs from that
seen in several matched composition cells.} It is not a claim that AI discovery as such
makes a material's label more or less reliable, and we offer no confident explanation for
the direction of the effect.

\subsection{Chemistry-aware verification budgets}\label{sec:budget}

If flip rates are predictable from composition, a maintainer with a limited r$^2$SCAN
budget should not sample uniformly. The policy is specified as follows, so that it can be
reimplemented exactly. Half of the 8{,}429 prototype clusters are drawn uniformly at random
without replacement to form the estimation set, and all materials in the remaining clusters
form the evaluation set; \textbf{the split is by cluster, not by material}, so
near-duplicate structures never straddle the two, and splitting by material would leak
information and inflate the result. A flip rate is estimated for each
(element count, anion class, lanthanide, 3$d$) cell from the estimation set only. Cells
absent from the estimation set are assigned that set's global rate. Evaluation-set
materials are ranked by their cell's estimated rate in descending order; materials within a
cell are tied and are ordered arbitrarily by a stable sort, which is immaterial because the
catch rate depends only on how many members of each cell fall inside the budget. A budget
$b$ selects the first $\lfloor bN \rfloor$ materials. The procedure is repeated over 20
independent cluster-wise splits and we report the mean and standard deviation across them.
We apply no shrinkage to small-cell estimates; doing so would plausibly improve the policy
and we have not tested it.

structural similarity or uncertainty in the estimated cell rates would plausibly improve it.

\subsection{Robustness}

\paragraph{Threshold.} Flip rates were recomputed at thresholds from 0 to 50 meV/atom
(Appendix~\ref{app:threshold}). The ordering of anion classes holds at every threshold, so
the result is not an artefact of the zero cutoff. Reporting against threshold rather than
at a single cut follows \citet{reents2026}.

\paragraph{Reference set.} All conclusions hold against both the MP and MP+OQMD hulls; the
latter runs 2--4 points higher uniformly, with cell ordering preserved. The ``All''
reference set behaves differently and is treated in Section~\ref{sec:allref}.

\paragraph{Pseudopotentials.} GNoME used newer PAW potentials for Li, Na, Mg, Ge and Ga.
Materials containing those elements flip \emph{less} (11.0\% against 13.5\%), so this
difference does not generate the effect.

\subsection{Hull completeness, probed on both sides}\label{sec:allref}

hull-completeness bias on the GNoME side. The chemistry gradient survives it: oxides,
pnictides and borides remain highest and intermetallics remain lowest. But the spread
compresses, from a 2.1-fold ratio between oxide and intermetallic under the MP reference
to 1.4-fold under ``All.'' We therefore report the MP-referenced gradient as an upper
bound on the chemistry effect and the ``All''-referenced gradient as a lower bound,
conditional on the inference above. We kept the MP reference as primary because it is the
quantity the source paper reports and the one against which we validated the pipeline,
not because it gives the larger spread.

\paragraph{The Materials Project side can be inspected directly.} GNoME's hulls are not
released, but the Materials Project's are. For each of the 22{,}756 known materials in
Section~\ref{sec:known} we define r$^2$SCAN hull completeness as the fraction of GGA(+$U$)
entries in the material's chemical system and all of its subsystems, excluding the
material itself, that also carry an r$^2$SCAN thermo document. This is a coarse measure:
it counts every entry, not only the ground-state structures that define the hull, so it
understates the completeness that matters. It is nevertheless the first direct
measurement of the quantity, and it varies strongly with chemistry
(Table~\ref{tab:mpcompl}). Oxides have the least complete r$^2$SCAN hulls of any class and
intermetallics the most complete, the reverse of what the GNoME ``All'' comparison
suggests for GNoME's own hulls. Because incompleteness understates flips, the known
oxide flip rate in Table~\ref{tab:mpcompl} is the most understated and the intermetallic
rate the least; with complete hulls the oxide--intermetallic ordering on the Materials
Project side would widen, not close.

\section{Selection effects}\label{sec:selection}

Beyond the propensity analysis of Section~\ref{sec:ipw}, two coverage features constrain
what can be claimed.

\paragraph{Element count.} Coverage is 12.9\% for binaries, 31.8\% for ternaries and 31.4\%
for quaternaries, then collapses to 0.13\% for quinaries (229 of 177{,}392) and zero for
senaries (0 of 4{,}792). The r$^2$SCAN validation effectively stops at four elements.

This is consequential. GNoME presents discovery in combinatorial spaces of four or more
elements, and emergent generalization to five and six, as a central result
\citep{merchant2023}. Quinaries make up 32\% of the released dataset. That regime has no
meta-GGA validation. Our logistic model finds flip risk \emph{decreasing} with element
count, which would predict quinaries to be relatively safe --- but extrapolating a fitted
trend past the edge of the data is exactly the move this paper argues against, and we
decline to make it.

\paragraph{Distance to the hull.} Coverage declines monotonically toward the hull, from
26.1\% in the $[-0.1,-0.05]$ eV/atom band to 16.5\% in $[-0.01,0]$ and 0.3\% above zero.
Supplementary Note 2 of the source work states that disagreement concentrates near the
hull. The recomputed subset therefore under-samples precisely the region where flips are
most likely, and 13.0\% should be read as a lower bound on the population rate. The
propensity model in Section~\ref{sec:ipw} includes PBE decomposition energy as a covariate
and so partially corrects for this.

\paragraph{Censoring bounds.} Treating all unrecomputed materials as flipped, then as
unflipped, gives bounds of 3.3\% to 78.1\% across the full 554{,}054-entry release. These
are uninformative, and we report the IPW estimate of 12.7\% conditional on the
$\le$4-element population instead.

\paragraph{Unmatched identifiers.} 22{,}498 r$^2$SCAN material IDs do not appear in the
current summary file. Their composition profile matches the remainder and their flip rate
is similar (11.4\% against 13.4\%), consistent with a release-version mismatch. Including
or excluding them changes no conclusion.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{For users of generated catalogues.} A single reliability figure attached to a
database is not usable. A group screening GNoME for transition-metal oxide cathodes faces
roughly a 30\% rate of verdicts that r$^2$SCAN would overturn; a group screening for
intermetallics faces roughly 4\%. We suggest datasets of this kind ship a per-entry
disagreement flag rather than a global statistic.

\paragraph{For benchmark design.} Benchmarks evaluate MLIPs against a PBE hull treated as
ground truth \citep{riebesell2025}. Our results imply the noise in that ground truth is
chemistry-structured. A model that appears to underperform on transition-metal oxides may
be partly disagreeing with labels that are themselves functional-sensitive in that
chemistry. We do not claim this explains any particular benchmark result.

\paragraph{For MLIP training data.} Warford et al.\ argue that selectively applied $U$
splits a training set into incompatible surfaces \citep{warford2026}. Section~\ref{sec:u}
shows the resulting label disagreement is concentrated in exactly the elements they
identify. Datasets built at the r$^2$SCAN level from the outset, such as MatPES
\citep{kaplan2025} and MP-ALOE \citep{kuner2025}, avoid the issue.

\paragraph{What would settle the physics.} Our evidence is statistical. We show that
open-shell $d$ content predicts disagreement after controlling for the correction scheme,
band gap, structure, size and recomputation selection. We do not show \emph{why} the two
functionals disagree for these materials. Establishing that requires a targeted study of a
small number of systems at higher levels of theory.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Hull completeness is the largest partially resolved threat.} An r$^2$SCAN
convex hull omits any competing phase for which no r$^2$SCAN calculation exists, biasing
toward apparent stability and therefore \emph{understating} flip rates. The Materials
Project's mixed scheme guards against this by using r$^2$SCAN formation energies only
where every ground-state structure has been recomputed \citep{mpmixingdocs}, but the
pure r$^2$SCAN thermo type we query does not, and the GNoME decomposition energies we
analyse were computed by the dataset authors against hulls we cannot inspect.

We state the consequence precisely. If reference-phase coverage varies systematically with
chemistry, part of the gradient we attribute to functional disagreement could instead
reflect differential hull completeness. Section~\ref{sec:allref} provides a partial
measurement: under the more inclusive ``All'' reference set, which we infer to be more
complete, the oxide-to-intermetallic ratio falls from 2.1 to 1.4 while the ordering is
preserved. The gradient is therefore real but its magnitude depends on hull completeness,
and we report the MP-referenced and ``All''-referenced figures as upper and lower bounds.
On the Materials Project side, where completeness can be measured, it varies by anion
class in the direction that understates oxide flip rates most, and the open-shell $d$
excess is unchanged across completeness terciles. A direct test on the GNoME side would
require the dataset authors' hull construction, which is not released.

\paragraph{Neither functional is ground truth.} r$^2$SCAN is more accurate than PBE for
formation energies on average \citep{kingsbury2022prm}, but not uniformly: for transition
metals and intermetallics the two are comparable and PBE is sometimes better
\citep{kothakonda2023,bartel2019}, and for copper SCAN and r$^2$SCAN overstabilise the
Cu$^{2+}$ state relative to Cu$^{+}$ where PBE+$U$ reproduces experiment
\citep{bae2025}. A low flip rate for a chemistry, as we find for copper, therefore says
only that the two functionals agree there, not that either is right. The noise floor is substantial: experiment-versus-
experiment MAE is 0.082 eV/atom \citep{kirklin2015}, and MP2020 correction uncertainties
alone can move near-hull polymorphs across the boundary \citep{wang2021mp2020}.

\paragraph{Clustering is corrected but the proxy is imperfect.} Our prototype proxy is
(space group, anonymised stoichiometry). This will merge genuinely distinct structures
sharing a space group and stoichiometry pattern, and will split related structures that
relaxed into different space groups. A true prototype assignment, for instance via a
structure-matching algorithm, would be better. We mitigate this by showing the headline
result is unchanged under a coarser definition (Section~\ref{sec:cluster}), and note that
because merging inflates cluster size, our design effect is more likely an over- than an
under-correction.

\paragraph{Composition explains little individual variance.} Pseudo-$R^2 = 0.066$. The
model supports population-level triage, not per-material prediction.

\paragraph{The known-materials baseline is itself selected.} Four-element known cells are
small ($n=108$--$297$), and ``known'' means previously catalogued, including theoretical
entries.

\paragraph{Unexplained features.} Nickel's position at the global average despite receiving
$U$, and the triclinic excess, are both unexplained.

\paragraph{Scope.} Every number applies to GNoME materials with at most four elements that
were selected for r$^2$SCAN recomputation.

\section{Conclusion}

A single aggregate survival rate is not an adequate description of how reliable
AI-generated stability labels are with respect to a higher-fidelity functional. Resolved by
chemistry, the PBE$\rightarrow$r$^2$SCAN flip rate varies more than sevenfold across
composition cells and, among non-$f$ elements, roughly sevenfold across elements. The variation survives correction for non-independence among substitution-generated
structures, stratification by distance to the PBE hull, controls for band gap and
structure, and reweighting for recomputation selection; it compresses but persists under a
more complete reference hull. It is produced by a directional shift, not by noise: r$^2$SCAN
moves open-shell 3$d$ oxides and pnictides toward instability and plain intermetallics
toward stability. The empirical Hubbard-$U$ correction does not generate the effect and
partially suppresses it where applied, so the largest disagreements in the dataset are
open-shell $d$ elements in chemistry that receives no correction. Open-shell $d$ content is the strongest observed predictor of disagreement
across every specification we tested; we do not claim to have identified the physical
mechanism, and establishing one would require targeted higher-level calculations on a small
number of systems. Lanthanide content, the usual suspect, is not significant on average and
reverses sign with the anion. The comparison against previously catalogued materials
reverses direction depending on composition. Verification budgets should follow the
chemistry, and the compositional regime GNoME advertises most prominently remains
unvalidated.
